# The Burnham Style: A Field Guide

A breakdown of the rhetorical system behind Andy Burnham's LinkedIn posts (sample: 20 consecutive posts, Oct 2026, ranging from ~70 to ~600 words). The goal is to copy the **technique** for your own voice and your own subject matter, not to put words in his mouth.

---

## 1. The core engine (memorise this)

Almost every post runs on a five-beat loop:

| Beat | What it does | Typical form |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Hook** | Drops the reader into a lived moment or a direct question | "You get back to your car and…" / "Imagine…" / "If you've got X, this is for you." |
| **2. The unfairness** | Shows how the system punishes an ordinary person, with a concrete detail | One short scenario, then a fragment that twists the knife |
| **3. The verdict** | A blunt moral judgment followed by action | "It's not fair. So we're changing it." |
| **4. The fix** | What changes, with hard numbers | £, %, people affected, minutes, dates |
| **5. Humble kicker** | Admits limits, then ends on a callback or a slogan | "There's more to do." + an echo of the opening line |

If you only take one thing from this guide, take beat 3. Some version of **"[That's / It's] not fair. So [I'm / we're] changing it."** shows up again and again. It's his signature move.

---

## 2. Hooks: the six openers he rotates

1. **The second-person scene.** Put the reader in a frustrating moment, in present tense.
   - Pattern: *"You [ordinary action] and [annoying thing happens]."*
2. **"Imagine…"** A thought experiment with emotional weight (a new baby, back at work the next morning).
3. **The call-out.** *"If you've got a [situation], this is for you."* Then three specific examples of who that is.
4. **The big question.** *"What kind of country do we want to be?"*, or a quirky one: *"What does [X] have in common with [Y]?"*
5. **The family story.** A named relative, a physical object, a specific place. It's personal and concrete, and it doesn't feel self-pitying.
6. **Pre-empting the eye-roll.** *"I know, you've heard me say this time and time again, but…"* This turns repetition into conviction.

**Rule:** the first line is always one sentence and stands as its own paragraph. No preamble, no "Excited to share".

---

## 3. Sentence-level mechanics

### Line breaks
- **One idea per paragraph.** Most paragraphs are 1–2 sentences, and a 3-sentence paragraph is "long".
- White space does the pacing. Leave a blank line between every paragraph.

### Fragments for punch
Right after a full sentence that explains a problem, he hits with a fragment:
- *"Not some of it. All of it."*
- *"Not even close."*
- *"Devolution by default."*

Formula: **"Not [the softer version]. [The harsh truth]."**

### Conjunction starts
Sentences often begin with **But / And / So / Because**. Each one marks a gear change:
- **But** = turn from problem to hope, or from progress to "more to do"
- **So** = the verdict leads to action
- **Because** = the moral reason, often a standalone sentence near the end
- **And** = stacking, often on the last item of a list ("And - crucially - …")

### Contractions everywhere
*I'm, we're, it's, don't, won't, that's, you'll.* He never writes "We are introducing" when "We're introducing" fits. (Exception: he slows down for emphasis, e.g. "That is how we bring back hope.")

### Spaced hyphens as dashes
He writes ` - ` (a hyphen with spaces) far more than an em dash. Use it for asides: *"money in your pocket - right when you need it most."*

### Triads and human lists
Benefits are shown through **three named kinds of people**, not abstractions:
> the parent on the school run. The small business… The cyclist…

Each one gets its own fragment and its own line.

### Anaphora for list posts
When announcing multiple things, every line starts the same way:
> I'm making… / I'm stopping… / I'm cracking down on… / I'm also ending…

or (budget-style) a run of **gerunds**: *Creating… Using… Making… Tripling… Extending… Lifting…*

## 4. Vocabulary

### Signature phrases (use sparingly, at most 1–2 per post)
- **for good** (bills down for good, off the streets for good)
- **breathing space**
- **hope** (the brand word, often the last word of the post)
- **every postcode** / **no postcode left behind**
- **fixing the basics**
- **common sense approach**
- **money in your pocket**
- **ripped off** / **rip-off** / **cowboy builders**
- **for too long**
- **I won't stop until…**
- **I'm getting on with it.**
- **There's more to do.** / **But I know there is more to do.**

### The villain
There is always a system-level antagonist, never a named individual: **Westminster**, "the system", "short-termism", rip-off companies, "the way things have always been done". The reader is always on the same side as the writer.

### Register
- Plain Anglo-Saxon words: *fix, help, cheap, fair, home, job, bills*.
- Concrete household nouns: windscreen, school run, engagement ring, Vinted, dog walking.
- No jargon unless he immediately translates it ("strategic authorities" is followed by what it means for you).
- British spelling and £ throughout.

### Avoid (these break the voice)
- Corporate filler: *leverage, synergy, excited to announce, thrilled, game-changer, delighted*
- Hashtags (he uses none)
- Emojis (only ✅ bullets once, in a dense list post)
- Exclamation marks (essentially zero; the conviction comes from short sentences, not punctuation)

---

## 5. Numbers

Every policy post has **at least two concrete figures**, used like evidence:
- How many people are affected ("around 400,000 people")
- What it's worth to one person ("an average £380", "up to £194 extra a week")
- Before/after limits (£1,000 → £3,000; 5 minutes / 10 minutes)

**Pattern:** the scale number makes it matter, then the per-person number makes it real. Always translate a big number into one person's pocket.

---

## 6. The humility clause (non-negotiable)

Nearly every post includes an explicit admission that the job isn't finished. This is what stops it reading like spin:
- *"It's a small measure, but…"*
- *"None of this fixes everything overnight. I know that."*
- *"I'm not pretending [X] will fix everything."*
- *"One [X] won't fix everything, and I won't pretend it will."*

Place it **right before the close**, then pivot to resolve: *"But… I won't stop until…"*

---

## 7. Closers

Pick one:
1. **Callback.** Echo the hook's exact wording. (Hook: "it can ruin your day" → Close: "That's one less thing to ruin your day.")
2. **Possessive twist.** *"Your [thing] should be exactly that - yours."*
3. **Two-part slogan.** Two parallel fragments: *"Breathing space today. Real hope for tomorrow."*
4. **The vow.** *"And I won't stop until it's fixed."*
5. **The vision line.** *"That's the fairer Britain I want to build."* → swap in your own vision noun.
6. **The terse status.** *"A lot done so far. But a lot to do. I'm getting on with it."*

---

## 8. "I" vs "we"

- **"I"** for conviction, emotion and personal accountability: *I just don't think it's fair. I've heard from so many…*
- **"We"** for delivery and scale: *We're introducing… We've tripled…*
- A typical post moves **I (feeling) → we (action) → I (promise)**.

---

## 9. The five post formats

### A. The Everyday Unfairness Fix (most common, ~120–250 words)
Hook scene → unfairness detail → fragment → "It's not fair. So we're changing it." → fix + numbers → humility → callback.

### B. The Personal Story (~250–350 words)
Named relative + object + place → what the system did to them → "I made a promise" → "That was [X] years ago. If anything, it's worse today." → "I know so many of you have lived this." → triad of shared experiences → the fix → vow.

### C. The Explainer / Round-up (~150–400 words)
"Here's everything…" or "What does [X] mean for you?" → one short framing principle ("[Budgets] are about making choices") → anaphora or ✅ list → humility → slogan.

### D. The Milestone Manifesto (~400–600 words)
"[N] days ago I…" → the promises → "There were plenty of people who told me this would be impossible" → three paragraphs of progress (big issues / everyday things) → "I'm not pretending the job's done" → "This is just the beginning. So stick with us."

### E. Addressing Concerns Head-On (~300–450 words)
Common-sense claim → "I know some of you will have concerns and questions, so I wanted to address them directly." → the decision → "But I want to reassure you…" → "The truth is…" → two aims → "Because this is how we can:" + one-line aims → "And - crucially - [the thing people care about most]."

## 10. Quick checklist before you post

- [ ] First line stands alone and is under 20 words
- [ ] No paragraph longer than 3 sentences
- [ ] At least one "Not X. Y." fragment or similar punch
- [ ] An explicit fairness verdict + "So…"
- [ ] At least two concrete numbers (scale + per-person)
- [ ] A humility clause near the end
- [ ] Closer is a callback, slogan or vow
- [ ] Zero hashtags, zero exclamation marks, no corporate filler
- [ ] Contractions throughout, British spelling
- [ ] Someone ordinary is named by situation (the landlord, the first-time investor), not by demographic

## 11. Why it works (and where it won't transfer)

It works because **every post is a small story with a moral**: someone ordinary, something unfair, a decision, a number, and an honest caveat. The humility clause buys credibility, and the short lines make it readable on a phone.

Where it won't transfer cleanly: "So I'm changing it" only lands if **you actually changed something**. Burnham can say it because he controls policy. For a founder or professional, the equivalent is shipping a feature, changing your pricing, fixing a process, or publishing data. If you use the fairness verdict without a real action behind it, the format reads as empty posturing very quickly.
